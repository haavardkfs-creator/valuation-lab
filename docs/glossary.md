# Glossary

Every metric in this project, explained in plain language. Haavard writes these in his own
words. Claude checks them for accuracy and says what is wrong, but does not write them for
him — the point is that each one can be explained out loud in an interview.

## Free cash flow (FCF)

The cash a company generates from its operations after taxes and after paying for the
investments needed to keep the business running (capex and working capital), but before
paying lenders or shareholders. It is the money that can be used to repay debt, buy back
shares, make acquisitions or build cash reserves.

## Discounted cash flow (DCF)

A valuation method that estimates what a company is worth today by forecasting its future
cash flows and discounting them back to the present. In this model, Adobe's DCF value
implies a share price of $305.60.

## Discount rate

The rate used to convert future cash flows into today's dollars. A higher discount rate
lowers the value of future cash flows, because investors require a higher return.

## WACC

Weighted Average Cost of Capital: the average return required by everyone who provides
capital to the company, shareholders and lenders, weighted by how much of each the company
uses. Adobe is funded about 94% by equity and 6% by debt, and its WACC is about 10.9% in
this model.

## Cost of equity

The return shareholders expect for taking the risk of owning the company's stock. In this
model it is calculated with CAPM (risk-free rate + beta × equity risk premium) and is about
11.4% for Adobe.

## Cost of debt

The effective interest rate a company pays on its borrowings. It is usually lower than the
cost of equity, because lenders are paid before shareholders if a company runs into
trouble, and because interest is tax-deductible. Adobe's is 4.16% before tax and about
3.4% after tax.

## Beta

A measure of how much a stock tends to move together with the overall market. Adobe's beta
of 1.38 means that when the market has moved 10%, Adobe has tended to move about 14% in the
same direction.

## Terminal value

The value of all cash flows beyond the explicit forecast period, forever. In this model it
represents about 70% of Adobe's enterprise value, making it the largest part of the
valuation.

## Terminal growth rate

The long-term growth rate assumed after the forecast period ends. It must stay at or below
long-run economic growth, because no company can outgrow the economy forever, and well
below WACC. This model uses 2.5%.

## Enterprise value (EV)

The value of the entire business, regardless of how it is financed. It represents the value
available to both lenders and shareholders, and is about $125.8 billion in this DCF.

## Equity value

The portion of a company's value that belongs to shareholders after accounting for debt and
cash. In this model Adobe's equity value is about $126.2 billion, slightly above enterprise
value because Adobe has more cash than debt.

## Net debt

What a company owes lenders minus the cash it has. If it is negative, like Adobe's
−$385 million, the company has more cash than debt.

## Implied share price

The value per share produced by a valuation model: equity value divided by shares
outstanding. In this DCF, Adobe's implied share price is $305.60.

## EV/EBITDA

A valuation multiple that compares a company's enterprise value to its EBITDA. It shows how
much investors are willing to pay for each dollar of operating earnings before interest,
tax, depreciation and amortisation.

## P/E

The price-to-earnings ratio compares a company's share price to its earnings per share. It
shows how much investors are willing to pay for one dollar of profit. Earnings are after
interest, so P/E is paired with the share price (equity value), not enterprise value.

## EV/Sales

A valuation multiple that compares a company's enterprise value to its revenue. It is
useful when profits are low or negative, or when companies have very different margins.

## Sensitivity analysis

A test that shows how the valuation changes when key assumptions change. In this model, the
share price is recalculated for different WACC and terminal growth rates to see which
assumptions matter most.
## Upside vs market
The percentage difference between the DCF price and the current market price. A positive value means the model estimates the stock is worth more than its current market price, suggesting potential undervaluation.

## Revenue growth
The rate at which a company’s sales increase over time. Higher revenue growth often signals stronger demand for the company’s products or services.

## Operating margin
Operating income divided by revenue. It shows how much profit a company keeps from its core business operations before interest and taxes.

## Peer median
The middle value of a metric for a group of comparable companies. It is often used as a benchmark when evaluating a company’s valuation or performance.

## DCF vs multiples
The percentage difference between the DCF price and the price implied by peer valuation multiples. A negative value means the DCF valuation is lower than the multiples-based valuation, while a positive value means it is higher.
